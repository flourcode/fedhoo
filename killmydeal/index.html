How to qualify a federal deal in five questions

Most federal pipeline dies for one of five reasons, and a seller usually knows which one before the meeting starts. The point of naming them is to stop pretending otherwise.

CUSTOMER — has anyone said out loud that they want this?

A requirement you inferred from a conversation is not a requirement. What you are looking for is the customer describing the problem in their own words, ideally in writing. If the only person who has articulated the need is you, the deal is a hypothesis.

MONEY — where does the funding come from?

Federal money has a name: an appropriation, a program line, an O&M budget, a color of money with a fiscal year attached. "They have budget" with no source is the most common thing a seller believes and cannot defend. The question your manager will ask is who told you, and what is it called.

POWER — have you met anyone who can make it happen?

Understanding the requirement and controlling the acquisition are different jobs, often different people, sometimes different buildings. A technical evaluator can love you and still not move a dollar. Progress means a program manager, a contracting officer, or a budget holder.

PATH — how would they actually buy it?

Every federal purchase arrives through a mechanism: an existing contract, a GWAC or IDIQ, a set-aside, a recompete, a sole-source justification. If nobody has named the vehicle and the contracting office, the timeline is guesswork, because acquisition lead time is the timeline.

NOW — what forces action this year?

Agencies are very good at doing nothing for another year. A forcing function is specific: a contract expiring, a product going end of life, a mandate with a date, an incident, or money that expires September 30. Without one, the deal is real and also not happening yet.

What actually happens in a pipeline review

A pipeline review is not a status meeting. It is a manager testing whether the numbers on the board can survive a question. Most sellers lose those meetings the same handful of ways.

Pattern

Happy ears

An enthusiastic conversation gets recorded as intent. Nothing the customer said commits them to anything.

Pattern

Phantom funding

The deal is forecast at a date nobody can tie to an appropriation or a fiscal year.

Pattern

No acquisition path

A real requirement with no vehicle, no contracting office, and therefore no plausible award date.

Pattern

Selling to the user

Deep relationships one layer below anyone who can move money or start an acquisition.

The difference between a seller who survives the review and one who does not is rarely deal quality. It is whether they walked in already knowing which of these applies to them.

For sales managers

Five questions, asked out loud, do more than a CRM field ever will. Run them on any deal in the review and you get a shared vocabulary instead of an argument: "I'm good on customer and path, I'm yellow on money and power."

  • Ask the same five every time. The consistency is what makes the answers comparable.
  • Let the rep answer before you react. Sort of is an honest answer and should be safe to give.
  • Weakest link first. The worst answer is the one that determines the forecast, not the average.
  • Nothing gets typed into a system. Nobody has to explain later why the pipeline looked bad in writing.

Questions

Does anything I enter leave my device?
No. The five answers are scored by JavaScript running in your browser. There is no account, no database, no analytics on your answers, and nothing is written to storage. The only network request this page makes is for its font.
Why doesn't it ask for the customer or the dollar value?
Because it doesn't need them, and asking would make the tool unusable for anyone whose employer restricts where pipeline detail can go. Qualification is about what you can prove, not who the customer is.
Is this AI?
No. It's a fixed rubric: each answer maps to a number, the numbers are weighted, three caps apply, and thresholds produce the verdict. The weights are visible in the page source. A language model given five words would invent context, and confident wrong advice is worse than none.
What do the four verdicts mean?
Healthy — real, positioned, and provable. Stable — probably real, but assertions outnumber facts. On life support — do not carry a date until something is confirmed. Dead on arrival — the procurement itself doesn't hold up.
Can managers use this in a pipeline review?
Yes, and that's the better use. Ask the five questions out loud, tap the answers as the rep gives them, and the verdict lands in front of both of you in under a minute. Nothing is recorded.
